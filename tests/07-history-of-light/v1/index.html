IFire · ≈ 1,800 K

Chapter I · c. 1,000,000 years ago

Fire

The first lamp was a hearth.

Long before anyone wrote the word light, our ancestors carried it. Burnt bone and plant ash in South Africa’s Wonderwerk Cave suggest that hominins were keeping fires roughly a million years ago; by four hundred thousand years ago, hearths appear across Africa, Europe and the Levant. Fire pushed back the night by a few metres — and in doing so, it invented the evening.

Firelight is the colour of wood coming apart. Soot particles heated to around a thousand degrees glow like tiny stars, and most of their radiance lands in the red and the infrared. It flickers because a flame is a turbulent negotiation between fuel, air and heat, renegotiated many times a second.

By that moving glow, people painted. Hand stencils on cave walls in Spain and Indonesia — some older than forty thousand years — were made in light that breathed.
≈ 1,800 K
colour temperature of a wood fire

Lower than any lamp that came after it: the warmest, reddest light humans have ever lived by.

IICandle · 1,850 K

Chapter II · c. 500 BCE – 1850

Candle

Fire, made portable and patient.

Romans dipped rope in tallow. Medieval churches burned beeswax, which smelled sweeter and cost a fortune. Eighteenth-century whalers sold candles of spermaceti so consistent that their brightness became a unit of measure — the ancestor of today’s candela. For two thousand years, an hour of reading had a price, and it was paid in wax.

In 1848 Michael Faraday gave his Christmas Lectures at the Royal Institution, later published as The Chemical History of a Candle. He showed children that the flame is hollow, that wax climbs the wick as a liquid and burns as a vapour, and that the yellow light is glowing soot.

“There is no better, there is no more open door by which you can enter into the study of natural philosophy, than by considering the physical phenomena of a candle.”Michael Faraday, 1848
1,850 K
a candle flame · about one candela

Deep amber, low and intimate: the light of letters, ledgers and late-night arithmetic.

IIIGas lamp · ≈ 2,200 K

Chapter III · 1792 – 1920s

Gas lamp

The night becomes public.

In 1792 the Scottish engineer William Murdoch lit his house in Redruth, Cornwall, with gas distilled from coal. In 1807 Frederick Winsor ran a demonstration line of gas lamps along London’s Pall Mall, and within two decades the city’s streets glowed on a network of iron pipes. Cities had begun to keep their own hours.

Every dusk the lamplighter walked his round with a long pole, opening valves and touching flame to burner; every dawn he walked it again to put them out. Then in 1885 Carl Auer von Welsbach patented the gas mantle — a fabric sock soaked in rare-earth salts that, once burnt away, left a ceramic lattice glowing a brilliant greenish white.

Gas lit the first night shifts, the first late shops, the first evenings that belonged to everyone.
2,000 → 2,700 K
open gas flame → incandescent mantle

The first time light got whiter by design rather than by luck.

IVBulb · 2,700 K

Chapter IV · 1879 – 2010s

Bulb

A wire, a vacuum, a switch.

Joseph Swan in Newcastle and Thomas Edison in Menlo Park both made carbon filaments glow inside evacuated glass at the end of the 1870s. Edison’s deeper invention was the system around the bulb: dynamo, meter, wiring, switch. In 1882 his Pearl Street Station began selling electric light to lower Manhattan by the hour.

Carbon gave way to tungsten in the early 1900s, and William Coolidge’s ductile tungsten wire of 1910 became the filament of the century. The principle never changed: push a current through a thin wire until it is hot enough to glow. More than ninety percent of the energy leaves as heat.

For a hundred years, the shape of an idea was a light bulb.
2,700 K
tungsten incandescent · “soft white”

We still name the warmth of modern lamps after the colour of a glowing wire.

VNeon · λ 640 nm

Chapter V · 1910 – today

Neon

Light learns to write.

In December 1910, at the Paris Motor Show, Georges Claude lit two long glass tubes filled with neon — a by-product of his business liquefying air. The colour was unlike anything a flame could make: not the glow of something hot, but the signature of a single gas, excited by electricity until it sang in red-orange.

Neon has no colour temperature in the usual sense. Each gas emits only certain wavelengths — neon its fierce reds, argon with a drop of mercury a cool blue — and phosphor coatings filled in the rest. By the 1920s glass benders were writing whole streets in light, from Paris to Los Angeles to Shanghai.

Light stopped being only illumination and became language.
λ 640 nm
neon’s brightest line, in the red

The first artificial light defined not by temperature but by atomic fingerprint.

VILED · 6,500 K

Chapter VI · 1962 – today

LED

Light from a crystal, cold to the touch.

In 1962 Nick Holonyak Jr., working at General Electric, built the first practical visible-light LED: a sliver of gallium arsenide phosphide that glowed red when electrons fell across a junction. For decades LEDs were indicator lights — on calculators, stereos, alarm clocks — because nobody could make a bright blue.

That changed in the early 1990s, when Isamu Akasaki, Hiroshi Amano and Shuji Nakamura grew high-quality gallium nitride and made blue LEDs bright enough to matter; they shared the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physics. Coat a blue chip in yellow phosphor and you get white.

“Incandescent light bulbs lit the 20th century; the 21st century will be lit by LED lamps.”Nobel Committee, 2014
6,500 K
cool daylight · 100+ lumens per watt

For the first time, artificial light can be any colour temperature we ask for.

VIILaser · λ 532 nm

Chapter VII · 1960 –

Laser

Light at its most disciplined.

On 16 May 1960, at Hughes Research Laboratories in Malibu, Theodore Maiman flashed a lamp around a small rod of synthetic ruby and produced the first laser pulse: deep red light at 694 nanometres, every photon in step with every other. Einstein had described stimulated emission in 1917; it took forty-three years to build it.

One colour, one direction, one phase. The laser was famously called “a solution looking for a problem”. Now it reads barcodes, carries the internet through glass fibre, cuts steel, reshapes corneas, and measures the distance to the Moon off mirrors left there by Apollo astronauts.

From a fire in a cave to a beam that touches the Moon: the same thing, learning to be precise.
λ 532 nm
a frequency-doubled green laser

A single wavelength, a single colour — the opposite of the fire we started with.

Coda

Every light in this story is still burning somewhere tonight: a campfire, a birthday candle, a restored gas lamp on a London street, a filament in a hallway, a sign over a bar, a screen, a beam. Each one changed the colour of the dark.
